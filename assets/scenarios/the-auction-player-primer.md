A player primer for the ideas discussed in **The Auction series**

A series of ideas for playing a one-page adventure in one of several ways. As intended, as a heist, as a mid-level social encounter or as a parlour Live Action Role Play or L(A)RP.

# Introduction

An evening of remarkable objects, careful manners and people who want
different things. This is a shared starting point for a tabletop role playing
game (RPG) or a small parlour Live Action Role Play (L(A)RP). It contains
player-facing material only.

<br />

### Your invitation

An auction brings together people with money, connections or a reason to be
near both. Attendants prepare the room, guests study the catalogue and the
auctioneer calls each lot. Between sales there is time to talk, make offers
and decide who deserves your trust.

You might be part of the security team, an uninvited visitor, a representative
acting for someone else, or a patron bidding in your own right.

Agree the version and your place in it with the Game Master (GM) or organiser before
play. The host can adapt the venue and the lots to the group's setting; this
primer does not establish a fixed catalogue or a guaranteed turn of events.

### What everyone can reasonably know

- The sale is a public occasion for those admitted, not an open invitation
  to every room in the building.
- Lots are introduced by the auctioneer. A description is a claim made by
  the seller or host, not proof of an object's history or qualities.
- Guests may value the same object for very different reasons. A losing bid
  need not end a conversation.
- Staff have jobs to do. Ask before handling a lot or entering a space marked
  as private, and expect your character's choices to be noticed.

### Make a connection

With another player's agreement, establish one connection before the sale:
an old favour, a shared acquaintance or a previous disagreement. Decide what
your characters know about it. Leave room for both people to surprise each
other in play.

## Who are you tonight?

Choose a viewpoint that fits the group's version of the auction. The prompts
are invitations to invent your own character, not secret instructions or
claims about other characters.

<br />
<br />

## Security

You are responsible for keeping the event orderly. Who hired you, and what
have you been asked to protect: people, property or the host's reputation?
Where do you draw the line if those duties pull in different directions?

### Uninvited visitor

You have a reason to be somewhere you are not expected. What are you hoping
to learn or recover, and what would make you leave without it? Agree with the
GM how your character has reached the occasion; do not assume access to
restricted areas.


### Proxy

You speak for an absent patron. What may you promise on their behalf? What
limit have they placed on your spending or your discretion? Name one thing
you want for yourself that is not in the brief.

### Patron

You have come in your own right. What brought you an invitation: wealth,
reputation, expertise or a useful relationship? What would you be prepared
to risk for a lot, and what would you rather keep than win?

# The shape of a sale

Listen as the auctioneer introduces a lot. Your character may have a role in presenting the lot; your character may want to bid, or be responsible for placing their bid on behalf of a patron.

Say so using the signal or procedure agreed for this event.

Keep track of your fictional budget and any authority you have to spend it.
The GM or organiser will explain increments, ties, payment and what happens if a bid
is disputed; do not assume that a promise or a favour counts as money.

Between lots, seek out a conversation.

You might ask about an object's
provenance, look for a partner in a bid, or make an offer that has nothing to
do with buying.

A question, a new acquaintance or a decision not to bid can
be as interesting as a purchase.

Maybe something else will draw your attention; remember your role in teh auction can be one of many.


### Reasons to pay attention

Use one of these open-ended prompts, or invent your own with the group:

- A family or community wants to know how a keepsake came to be offered.
- A scholar hopes to examine an unfamiliar instrument before it disappears
  into a private collection.
- A guest needs to speak with a rival without causing a public scene.
- A patron wants to be seen supporting a cause, whether or not they buy.
- A representative must choose between following instructions and preserving
  a relationship they value.

These are possible starting points, not statements about a particular lot or
person.

Ask the GM or organiser which fit the session.

# Above the table

Playing comfortably together


Before play, agree the tone, boundaries and a way to pause or leave a scene
without having to explain why.

Check in out of character if a scene becomes
uncomfortable.
Anyone can ask for a change; the group can pause, rewind or
move on.
Character conflict is not permission to disregard a player's
boundary.

## A few manners

Introduce yourself as much or as little as your character wishes.

Let the auctioneer be heard when a lot is called, and leave space for quieter
players to act.

You can challenge a claim, withdraw from a conversation or
decline a deal.

A character can be rude or deceptive without treating another player unkindly.

## Tabletop

For tabletop play, agree how the chosen game's rules handle social pressure,
money and conflict.

Describe what you want to do and let the GM explain when
a rule or roll is needed.

\column

## Parlour games or L(A)RP

For a parlour L(A)RP, use fictional money and clearly identified game props.

Do not bring real valuables into play. Agree before the event whether touch,
moving props, private conversations and simulated conflict are permitted.

Unless everyone has explicitly agreed otherwise, do not touch another
person or take their belongings.
A pause or an out-of-character departure always takes priority over staying in character.

Costume and performance experience are optional and aspirational.

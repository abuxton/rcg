There is a particular sort of optimism in putting a weekly game back in the
calendar. It is not quite the same as starting a brand new campaign. We have
been here before; we know how readily a brilliant first session can become an
uncertain "shall we see how next week looks?" and then a Discord server full
of old maps.

Fortunately, there is no unfinished campaign demanding a heroic recovery.
We managed to close the previous ones properly. That means this is not a
continuity exercise, an attempt to remember who owed which baron a favour, or
a compromise with an old plot. It is a chance to decide what we actually want
to play now.

The aim is modest: a reliable weekly table of four to six people, including
the GM. Before choosing a system, that means agreeing that a campaign should
earn a few sessions rather than assuming it deserves years. A short initial
arc, a regular time, and a clear way to say "I cannot make this week" sound
unromantic, but they are kinder to everyone than an endless and increasingly
guilt-ridden scheduling thread.

## A fresh pitch, not a resurrection

The useful question is not "what did we used to play?" but "what can this
group sustain and get excited about now?" Familiar fantasy is a perfectly
respectable answer, but it is not the only one. I have already made the case
that the hobby is much larger than *Dungeons & Dragons* in
[Beyond the Dungeon](https://redcirclegames.co.uk/posts/2026/05/09/beyond-the-dungeon-ttrpg-alternatives.html).
Restarting is a good moment to take that abundance seriously.

There are two broad directions. We can choose a game intended to carry a
weekly campaign, or we can use a few one-shots to find our appetite before
committing. Neither is less serious. A one-shot that leaves the table talking
all week is useful evidence; so is discovering that a much-loved system needs
more preparation than our real lives will allow.

### Candidates for the weekly slot

* [*Blades in the Dark*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/12) is
  especially tempting for a restart. A crew, a city, and a sequence of jobs
  give us a natural rhythm: plan a score, play through the consequences, and
  see what new trouble it creates. It promises a campaign that can grow
  sideways as well as forwards, and an absent player is easier to explain
  when the crew's business continues.
* [*Dragonbane*](https://redcirclegames.co.uk/posts/2026/06/14/dragonbane-free-league-review.html)
  offers a more traditional fantasy route without demanding that we begin by
  memorising a continent. Its Misty Vale setting and fast-moving play make it
  a strong candidate if we want exploration, monsters, and a shared map to
  fill in together.
* *Dungeons & Dragons* remains the obvious familiar option. The current
  [free rules](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/free-rules) make it easy
  to test the waters, and most of us already understand its basic grammar.
  Familiarity is valuable, provided we do not mistake it for an obligation to
  start an enormous open-ended campaign.
* [*Vaesen*](https://freeleaguepublishing.com/games/vaesen/) could split the
  difference: a recurring group of investigators tackling folklore mysteries
  in a shared setting. It feels well suited to a limited weekly series, but
  deserves a conversation about horror, folklore, and what sort of darkness
  the table wants.
* [*Spire: The City Must Fall*](https://rowanrookanddecard.com/product/spire-rpg/)
  has a wonderfully clear premise: a resistance fighting an occupying power.
  That focus could bring a committed group together quickly, but its politics
  and revolutionary themes make it a game to pitch honestly, not to spring on
  people between snacks.

My present instinct is that *Blades in the Dark* and *Dragonbane* are the
front-runners for a new weekly game, for almost opposite reasons. One gives
us a connected city and a crew with problems; the other gives us a focused
fantasy wilderness and room to explore. That is an opinion, not a verdict.
The first session should be a Session Zero: a chance to test the pitch,
establish safety and expectations, and agree what "weekly" really means for
the people in the room.

## The harder rule system is the calendar

A table of four to six, GM included, is large enough to feel lively and small
enough for every absence to matter. The old trap is requiring every person to
be present for every long session. Justin Alexander's
[writing on opening the game table](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1223/roleplaying-games/opening-your-game-table)
is useful here even if we are not running an open-table campaign: end at a
safe stopping point, keep a stable base or clear next objective, and let the
available players make the next outing work.

That does not mean attendance stops mattering. It means we can decide in
advance what quorum looks like, whether a character fades into the background
for a week, and when the GM will call a cancellation. A short first arc makes
that agreement easier. At the end we can ask whether the game, time, and
format deserve another arc rather than treating a pause as a failure.

## Discord as a campaign noticeboard

I have written before about
[Discord channels for tabletop gaming](https://redcirclegames.co.uk/2024/01/10/discord-for-tabletop-gaming.html).
For this restart I want to resist building a cathedral of empty channels.
Discord's text and voice tools are useful, as its own
[beginner's guide](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/360045138571-Beginner-s-Guide-to-Discord)
shows, but the purpose is communication, not a second hobby.

The minimum useful setup is an announcements channel for the fixed slot and
changes, a scheduling channel with a simple RSVP message for each session, a
table-chat channel, and a recap-and-notes channel. One voice room is enough
to start. A 24- or 48-hour RSVP deadline gives the GM time to prepare for the
table that will actually be there, rather than the theoretical one.

After each session, a few bullets covering what happened, who was present,
and what remains unresolved are more valuable than a heroic essay. The recap
is a handrail for players who missed a week and a small, accumulating record
of the campaign for everyone else.

## Where AI has changed the preparation, and where it has not

When I wrote [AI Assistants and Gaming](https://redcirclegames.co.uk/2024/06/01/AI-prompt-gms.html),
AI support was already useful for rough encounter lists, NPC prompts, and
early ideas, but unreliable when asked to produce rules-accurate game
material. That remains the important distinction. The tools are now more
capable at helping a GM turn a short campaign pitch into a list of questions,
a recap template, or several possible hooks. They still need a human to
decide whether any of it belongs at this table.

For a new campaign I would use AI as a private brainstorming and
administration assistant, not as a substitute GM and not as an authority on a
system's rules. It can help turn Session Zero notes into a concise Discord
post, suggest a few connective details between player characters, or produce
several plainly labelled options for a missed-session recap. It should not be
fed private player material without permission, asked to reproduce published
adventures, or trusted to invent monster statistics without checking the
actual rules. The social work -- listening, judging tone, and responding to
the people at the table -- remains ours.

## A shelf of future one-shots

Choosing one weekly campaign should not exile every other game to the
cupboard. A future one-shot is a good way to change genre without asking
anyone to abandon the main game.

[*Heart: The City Beneath*](https://rowanrookanddecard.com/product/heart-the-city-beneath-rpg/)
looks like a deliberately intense descent into strange fantasy, while
[*Vaesen*](https://freeleaguepublishing.com/games/vaesen/) can make a focused
folklore investigation. *Spire* may also work well as a self-contained look
at its remarkable city and resistance. Their tone makes an up-front
conversation about boundaries part of the invitation, rather than an
afterthought.

For a change of genre, [*Shadowrun Anarchy*](https://store.catalystgamelabs.com/products/shadowrun-anarchy)
looks like a practical, rules-light route into cyberpunk fantasy, while
[*Cyberpunk RED*](https://rtalsoriangames.com/cyberpunk/) offers a different
sort of neon future. [*ALIEN RPG*](https://freeleaguepublishing.com/games/alien/)
is almost an argument for a finite event by itself: tense science-fiction
horror is more satisfying when everyone has signed up for the same pressure
and length of story.

I have opened follow-up review issues for
[*Spire*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/68),
[*Heart*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/66),
[*Vaesen*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/65),
[*Shadowrun Anarchy*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/67), and
[*ALIEN RPG*](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/69). The plan is to play
them as one-shots, record what the table experiences rather than merely what
the books promise, and decide which deserve a fuller review.

For now, the next step is smaller than choosing the perfect game: put a
Session Zero in the calendar, present two or three honest pitches, and see
which one makes four to six people want to turn up next week.

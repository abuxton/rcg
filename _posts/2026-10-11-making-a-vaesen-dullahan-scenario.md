After running a Dullahan mystery for our Society investigators, I wanted to see
if I could turn the idea into something another Vaesen group could pick up and
play. Not a retelling of the session, and not a rewrite of a published
mystery: a compact, standalone scenario built from the situation that made our
game work.

The first session began with a hatbox marked by a black star arriving at the
Society house near Highgate Cemetery. A member of staff opened it and was
drenched in blood. Inside was a skull. The investigators followed the clues,
learned about the Dullahan, and worked out that the creature would come for its
head after dark. They had found useful information, but not the head's most
important secret. Rather than wait for a fight at the house, they lured the
Dullahan towards Highgate, left its head where it could reclaim it, and watched
from safety.

That session left me with a good starting point for a scenario: an immediate
threat with a clear desire, a deliberate act of intimidation by an unknown
antagonist, and a time limit that made the investigators choose what to do
with incomplete information.

## Researching the Dullahan

I returned to the Dullahan material in [*Vaesen: Mythic Britain & Ireland*](https://freeleaguepublishing.com/shop/vaesen-2/mythic-britain-ireland/),
particularly the creature entry on pages 60-61. The creature is more than a
headless rider to be fought: it is a deathly presence with its own rules,
behaviours, and vulnerabilities. The book's aged, obfuscated text on page 61
also supplied the secret that drove the new scenario: the head can be seized
to control the Dullahan, and destroying it destroys the creature.

That changed the shape of the problem. In our session, the investigators
didn't discover that secret before the Dullahan arrived, and they chose a
cautious solution. For a standalone scenario, I wanted the group to have a
chance to uncover the truth and decide what to do with it. Returning the head,
using it to control the Dullahan, and destroying it should all feel like
meaningful choices, rather than a single correct answer hidden behind a roll.

## Learning from the published mysteries

I also reviewed the three mysteries in *Mythic Britain & Ireland*: **Old
Meg**, **The Llantywyll Incident**, and **The Hampstead Group**. I used them as
examples of how the book organises an adventure for a GM, not as text or plot
material to reproduce. Each mystery has its own situation and play style, but
their presentation offers a useful framework: establish the background and
conflicts, give the investigators a reason to get involved, then support play
with clues, people, places, escalating events, a confrontation, and possible
aftermaths.

The differences between them were as instructive as the shared format. **Old
Meg** builds pressure through danger to the community and conflict between
locals and authorities. **The Llantywyll Incident** turns on a clash of
beliefs, livelihoods, and supernatural neighbours, with more than one way to
resolve the danger. **The Hampstead Group** layers a missing-person
investigation with social barriers, misleading accounts, and a concealed
predator.

Those examples helped me ask practical questions about the new scenario:
What can investigators learn? What might delay or complicate them? What
resources can they gather? What does the creature want? What changes if they
return, seize, destroy, or lose the skull? The resulting scenario borrows the
useful GM-facing organisation, but its plot and text are newly written around
our Dullahan premise.

## Turning the session into a one-page scenario

The biggest change was making the deadline explicit. The hatbox arrives
without warning, so there is no ordinary Society preparation before the
mystery starts. The investigators have a short daylight window to research and
gather gold. Sunset marks the end of relative safety; the Dullahan may arrive
at any point after dark, with midnight as the expected culmination rather than
a precise appointment.

I wanted that clock to create urgency without taking choices away. The
countdown advances when time is spent, the skull is exposed, or an investigation
goes badly—not simply because the players ask questions. The Dullahan's arrival
is foreshadowed by signs from its description, so the threat grows in the
fiction as well as on the clock.

The Society's headquarters is the scenario's only fixed location. Investigators
may decide to find gold, consult a contact, hide the skull, or lure the
Dullahan somewhere else. Leaving those places open gives the group room to
make a plan instead of forcing a tour through a list of prepared scenes.

I kept the essential clues available through several routes: Society records,
folklore knowledge, a specialist, or an NPC. A failed test can cost time or
create a complication, but it should not stop the mystery. The secret of the
head is what makes the final decision interesting, so it shouldn't disappear
because one roll went badly.

## A press pack worth exploring

While researching and preparing the article, I also looked through Free
League's [press page](https://freeleaguepublishing.com/press/). I was struck by
how useful the press material is for someone making something around the game:
the supplied *Mythic Britain & Ireland* banner is large enough to work as a
proper header, rather than a tiny logo or thumbnail. I used it both in the
scenario PDF and here, linking back to the press pack and crediting the source.
That kind of ready-to-use visual material makes it much easier to give a fan
project a coherent look without having to invent artwork of my own.

The finished [one-page scenario, *The Head in the Hatbox*](/assets/scenarios/the-head-in-the-hatbox.pdf),
is available as a PDF. I have also kept its Markdown source alongside it in
the site's assets so it can be revised and republished later.

The earlier session write-up is here: [A Dullahan, a Deadline, and a Night at
Highgate](https://redcirclegames.co.uk/posts/2026/10/04/vaesen-dullahan-highgate-session-review.html).

This little project has been a satisfying loop: play a mystery, think about
what made it work, research the folklore and the published adventure format,
then make a new scenario that leaves the decisions to the next group.

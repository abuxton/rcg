In [*The Auction for patrons: when the party has its own
money*]({{ '/2026/09/23/the-auction-for-established-adventurers/' | relative_url }}),
I suggested that high-level characters are interesting at an auction because
the room already knows who they are. A lock is rarely the problem. Being seen
opening it is.

That makes the same premise a strong third iteration of the auction as a
parlour Live Action Role Play (L(A)RP). The earlier proxy game put delegates
in the room on somebody else's behalf. This version asks players to be the
people whose names are on the invitations: veterans, patrons, diplomats and
collectors with enough history that every public choice has a cost.

## What this version inherits

The series began with [**a review of the one-page
adventure**]({{ '/2026/09/02/the-dwarven-auction-one-page-review/' | relative_url }}),
then looked at [**the auction as a
heist**]({{ '/2026/09/09/the-auction-as-a-heist/' | relative_url }}) and
[**the auction by proxy**]({{ '/2026/09/16/the-auction-proxies-and-parlour-larp/' | relative_url }}).
All three are useful preparation for an organiser, even though this event has
no Game Master behind a screen.

From the security version, I would keep the timetable and the sense that
several problems are moving at once. From the heist, I would keep incomplete
information, preparations that can pay off and a reason not to solve every
problem with force. From the proxy game, I would keep private briefs and the
understanding that a bid is a social act, not merely a number.

The patrons version adds the essential final ingredient: the characters'
reputations belong in the room before their players do. It is possible to
arrive with a fine coat, a famous name and a large purse, yet still be unable
to bid without admitting something uncomfortable.

## Build roles around reputation

For a first event, I would plan for eight to twelve players, an auctioneer and
one organiser who can answer practical questions. Every player needs a
character brief with four clear elements:

* a public identity that the other guests can recognise;
* one lot, person or outcome they actively want;
* leverage they can offer, threaten or trade; and
* a line they do not want crossed in public.

Those elements give a player a reason to initiate scenes. “The retired
adventurer” is an interesting costume idea; “the retired adventurer who must
stop a sword being sold because it proves their old company abandoned a town”
is a playable role.

The roles should overlap rather than divide into heroes and villains. The
temple envoy might want a relic returned, but need the discreet collector's
funding. The collector might know that the relic was stolen, but need the
envoy's silence about a different lot. The host might be trying to protect the
auction house's reputation while quietly discovering which guest leaked the
catalogue.

Give every player at least two relationships before play begins. One can be
public and uncomplicated; the other should create a decision. A debt, old
friendship or shared failure is more immediately useful than a page of setting
history.

## Make the lots do the work

I would use three or four lots, not a catalogue full of imagined magic items.
Each should have a visible description, a public provenance and a private
complication. A small card beside the prop is enough. The object itself need
not be elaborate: a labelled box, a ribboned scroll case or a carefully chosen
piece of costume jewellery will let players focus on what it means.

The private complication is where the play lives. Perhaps the provenance is
false, a family has a better claim, or an item can only be used by somebody
who would be embarrassed to own it. Different briefs can reveal different
parts of the truth. No one needs the organiser to announce the answer; the
room should be able to bargain, expose, conceal or live with it.

Do not ask players to spend real money. Give each character a small number of
bid tokens and one or two non-monetary favours they may offer. A debt forgiven,
a future introduction or public support for a claim can matter as much as a
token. The auctioneer can record the final bid on a card so that the result is
visible and later consequences have something concrete to refer to.

## Give the evening a rhythm

A two-and-a-half to three-hour event needs a firm public structure and roomy
private time. I would begin with a short out-of-character briefing, then let
guests arrive and circulate before the first lot. Call a lot approximately
every half hour. Between them, announce a break, a fresh rumour, a delivery or
a request for a word in private.

The auctioneer's job is not to perform every scene. They keep time, call bids
and make the room listen when public attention matters. An organiser can
quietly place a sealed note, answer a rules question or introduce a
complication when play has stalled. Those interventions should offer a choice,
not reveal a plot that the players must follow.

For a final beat, I would choose one question that affects everybody: will the
guests support the host after the disputed provenance comes to light, allow a
claimant to take a lot, or expose the person responsible? End with a public
decision, then give players a few minutes to make promises for after the
auction. The best ending is usually a changed relationship, not a winner
announced by the organiser.

## Treat safety as part of the design

Status, debt and public humiliation can be excellent fictional pressure, but
they can also land too close to home. Before play, explain the tone, physical
contact rules, whether props may be taken, and how private conversations work.
My default would be no physical conflict, no real valuables in play and no
expectation that anyone drinks alcohol or wears a costume.

Offer a simple, agreed way to pause, step out of a scene or signal that its
intensity should change. Make an organiser visibly available for practical or
emotional support. A short debrief after the final scene also lets people
separate the character who drove a hard bargain from the player who enjoyed
the game.

This is the same work I would do in a
[Session Zero]({{ '/2023/12/03/session-zero/' | relative_url }}): establish
the shared boundaries that make sharper fictional choices possible. The
auction only needs a dressed room, clear props and good briefs. Its real
scenery is the trust that lets players risk status without risking one another.

## A small event with long consequences

The patron version works because it turns an established campaign's background
into immediate play. An old ally can be across the room. A vague obligation
can become the price of a lot. A reputation can become something a player must
protect, spend or deliberately discard.

That is a useful L(A)RP lesson beyond auctions. Give people a public occasion,
a reason to want incompatible things and enough shared history for the choice
to matter. The room will supply the adventure.

Some adventures arrive with a great deal of setting, a cast list, a plot
diagram and a small weather system. *The Dwarven Auction* arrives with one
page and an excellent question: can a group of temporary security staff keep
an auction of remarkable weapons from going disastrously wrong?

It is a very compact premise, but it has the important ingredients already in
motion. There is a prestigious venue, expensive objects, staff who know more
than they are saying, guests with private motives and a clock running towards
the end of the sale. The original breaks that hour into successive beats, so
the Game Master has permission to put pressure on the players rather than wait
for them to find a plot.

## A strong use of a small map

The plan is not just decoration. Its few rooms establish public and restricted
space, a narrow route through the building and one deliberately unexplained
door. That is enough geography for a party to make meaningful patrol choices.
It also makes each incident legible: when a lot moves from display to sale,
who saw it? When somebody acts strangely, where can they go next?

I particularly like that the scenario's trouble comes from several directions.
There are opportunists, a member of staff with a temptation, a more personal
claim on one of the lots, and an intrusion that is neither polite nor
negotiable. None needs a large villainous monologue. The players are simply
asked to work out what matters most before the next problem arrives.

The result feels useful as an opening-night RPG. The party can be assembled as
contract staff without a contrived shared history, then finish with a choice:
take their pay and leave, investigate the association that hired them, or
follow one of the loose ends created during the sale.

## The gaps are invitations

The most productive thing about a one-page adventure is often what it declines
to settle. Who founded the association? Why is one room sealed? How did the
collection acquire these things, and what does it mean to sell them? Those are
not omissions I need to repair before play. They are handles a GM can turn
towards their own setting and players.

There are practical choices to make, of course. I would decide before the
session what the security team's limits are, how much force suits the campaign,
and what a successful evening looks like. I would also choose a single
answer for any ambiguity in the rooms rather than treating every hint as a
mandatory encounter. A short adventure needs room to breathe.

That open structure is what made me start sketching variations. The next posts
look at the same auction house from the other side of the locks: first as a
heist, then as a web of proxies and finally as a gathering for established
adventurers.

## Credit where it belongs

This is a review and response to *The Dwarven Auction*, a one-page adventure
written and illustrated by **Nimrod Cortés** and published by
**[OR15 Publishing](https://www.or15.com/)**. The original remains the
property of OR15 Publishing; this post does not reproduce its text, artwork or
map. It is discussed here with respect for its personal-use terms.

The compact-adventure format also belongs in the wider tradition encouraged by
the **[One Page Dungeon Contest](https://www.dungeoncontest.com/)**. Its work
is worth exploring if, like me, you enjoy adventures that make every bit of
page space earn its place.

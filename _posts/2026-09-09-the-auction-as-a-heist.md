Last week I wrote about why *The Dwarven Auction* works so well from the
security desk. This week I am taking the less respectable route: what happens
when the players arrive the night before with a target, partial intelligence
and somebody else's money?

The useful change is not simply swapping guards for thieves. It changes the
question at the table. A security adventure asks, "Which threat do we answer
now?" A heist asks, "What do we learn, what do we risk, and which way out will
still be open when this goes wrong?"

## Give the job a human reason

In my notes, the crew is after one particular lot. It has a disputed history,
and the client wants it recovered without turning a private grievance into a
public battle. That gives the party a reason to be careful. They need not be
heroes, but indiscriminate violence would make them bad at the job.

I would tell the players the objective, the deadline and the information their
client has already bought. Then I would let the plan belong to them. A roof,
a courtyard, an old service route, a front door that merely looks too obvious:
each can be a credible approach if it creates a different problem afterwards.
The trick is not to hide the one correct entrance behind a difficult roll.

The auction house needs a little expansion for this version: exterior space,
staff routines, somewhere deliveries once moved through, and a believable
reason that security changes between day and night. Those additions should
support choices rather than become a larger map for its own sake.

## Preparation should pay off

I like heists best when a player can make a clever preparation matter after the
plan hits reality. Rather than demand that they name every tool in advance, I
would ask useful questions during play. Did anyone study the shift pattern?
Who knows a guest or supplier? What did the client omit? A sensible answer can
create an advantage, a new complication, or both.

The original auction is already rich with complications: valuable containers,
unsettled staff, magical security and people whose interests do not line up.
For a heist I would use only a few at once. A guard patrol is enough until the
party creates noise; an unusual display is enough until somebody touches it.
Escalation works better than putting every danger in every room.

The most satisfying conclusion is not necessarily a clean escape. The crew
might deliver the lot and discover their client represents an old criminal
organisation. They might leave a rival with proof of their involvement. They
might find that the auction house is not simply a victim, but a collector with
its own uncomfortable history. Any of those can become the next session.

## The design lesson

This variation has made me think of a map as a promise. A narrow corridor
promises a patrol. A sealed room promises a secret. A high window promises
someone will want to climb it. The GM does not need to force the promised
scene; they need to make it possible for the players to pursue it.

That is why the small original premise expands so easily. The place remains an
auction house, but changing who has authority and who has a plan creates a
different game entirely.

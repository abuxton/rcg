Finishing a scenario is a satisfying moment. Finishing one that another group
might actually run creates a more awkward question: what would it take to put
it in front of strangers?

I have been asking that about [*The Head in the Hatbox*]({{
'/assets/scenarios/the-head-in-the-hatbox.pdf' | relative_url }}), the small
Vaesen mystery I made from a Dullahan session. It is already a PDF, which
makes a DriveThruRPG listing sound pleasantly straightforward. In practice,
the PDF is only one part of the job. The more important question is whether I
have permission to make and sell the thing it contains.

This is not legal advice, and I am not announcing a product. It is the
beginning of a checklist for anyone, including me, who has a scenario, player
primer, or other game handout and wonders whether it could become a listing.

## There is more than one front door

[DriveThruRPG's guide to creating and selling a PDF](https://help.drivethrurpg.com/hc/en-us/articles/33395033102615-How-to-Create-and-Sell-a-PDF)
separates three routes that are easy to muddle together:

- A Publisher or Partner account is for work where I control the relevant
  rights, including a completely original game or setting.
- A Community Content Program is for making work with a particular publisher's
  game and setting under that programme's agreement.
- Dungeon Masters Guild is its own Dungeons & Dragons-focused programme, not
  a general licence for other games.

That distinction changes everything. A Partner account is not a magic key to
another publisher's setting, rules, logos, or artwork. It gives a creator a
way to sell their own work; it does not supply the rights that work might
need.

For *Vaesen*, the relevant programme is [Free League Workshop](https://help.drivethrurpg.com/hc/en-us/articles/12723258160919-Free-League-Free-League-Workshop).
Its public guidance includes *Vaesen – Nordic Horror Roleplaying* amongst the
supported games. It says a normal DriveThruRPG customer account is enough to
start, provided the creator accepts the Community Content Agreement.

That makes it a much more promising route for a Vaesen-compatible scenario
than assuming a normal publisher listing would be enough. It is also a
reminder that the agreement, rather than a hopeful interpretation of a shop
category, is the document that matters.

## A listing is not just the file

The PDF still matters. DriveThruRPG's [Product Standards Guidelines](https://help.drivethrupartners.com/hc/en-us/articles/12780748778135-Product-Standards-Guidelines)
expect a cover, a useful description, and a product that tells a prospective
reader what system it uses and what they will receive. Its [title setup
guidance](https://help.drivethrupartners.com/hc/en-us/articles/12780799278103-Set-Up-a-New-Title-or-Edit-an-Existing-Title-Listing)
also makes the ordinary work visible: author and artist credits, page count,
price, categories, cover art, a preview, and a careful description.

None of that is especially mysterious, but it does suggest a useful shift in
mindset. I would not upload the first PDF that happens to be on my computer. I
would read it as a customer: can I tell what it is, whether it is for my game,
and what I am buying before I reach page one?

The standards also say that marketplace products must be human-written. They
set specific rules for generative art and require the appropriate creation
method tag when it is present. That is another part of preparation, not a
small detail to leave until the upload screen.

## What Free League Workshop adds

The Free League Workshop rules are more specific because they make use of
someone else's game possible. The published guidance says that creators keep
half of the proceeds. It also requires the Free League Workshop logo on the
cover and the game's required legal notice in the product.

Most importantly for my own scenario, it says not to reuse artwork from Free
League products unless that use is expressly allowed. Public-domain work,
properly licensed stock art, commissioned art with commercial permission, and
Creative Commons material that permits commercial use are possible starting
points. They are not interchangeable labels: every image needs its own
provenance checked.

So the question for *The Head in the Hatbox* is not merely whether its pages
look presentable. Before I could consider a listing, I would need to:

1. Read the current Community Content Agreement at sign-up rather than relying
   only on a summary article.
2. Find the Vaesen-specific legal wording and the current programme logo.
3. Audit every illustration, texture, font, and other asset against the
   programme's permitted sources.
4. Check the PDF itself for readability, credits, and a cover that works at
   thumbnail size.

There is a useful discipline in that list. Marketplace requirements and
permission questions belong beside each other, but they are not the same job.
A polished file cannot grant a licence, and a valid licence cannot rescue a
confusing product page.

## The small first step

I find the scale of a marketplace listing less intimidating when I treat it
as a sequence of questions rather than a leap into publishing. Is this an
original piece of work? Which route actually covers it? Can I account for
every asset? Does the PDF help a GM run the thing? Can I explain the answer
in a few honest lines?

For now, the answer for my scenario is that there is more checking to do. That
is still progress. It gives me a proper next step: read the binding terms,
review the assets, and decide whether the result deserves another editing
pass before I worry about a price or a product page.

I will keep the source links from this research nearby, because platform terms
change. If I ever reach the point of submitting something, I will check the
current guidance again rather than treating this post as a permanent rulebook.

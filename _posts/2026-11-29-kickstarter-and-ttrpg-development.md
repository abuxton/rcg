I have backed a fair few things on [Kickstarter](https://www.kickstarter.com/).

This is not a confession. It is not even the worst thing I have done before
coffee. I have never bought a second-hand canal boat because somebody on the
internet promised it was "almost seaworthy". My Kickstarter habit is therefore
probably fine.

Kickstarter is a crowdfunding platform where creators present a project,
usually with a funding target and a deadline, and ask people to pledge money
to help make it happen. If the campaign reaches its target, the project can go
ahead. Backers normally receive a reward or a copy of the thing, although the
important bit is that they are supporting a project before it exists as a
finished product.

That last part is the interesting bit for tabletop Role Playing Games (TTRPGs).
The [TTRPG section of Kickstarter's games discovery pages](https://www.kickstarter.com/discover/categories/games/ttrpg)
is a constantly changing window into games, supplements, miniatures, journals,
magazines, adventures, and projects that are adjacent to gaming without fitting
neatly into a rulebook-shaped box.

It is also a very effective way of making me think, "That looks interesting,"
followed shortly afterwards by, "I shall have to eat slightly less ambitiously
this month."

## What is a pledge actually buying?

A Kickstarter pledge is not quite a normal purchase. It is not quite a
donation either. It sits in that awkward middle ground where everybody is
trying to be sensible while looking at concept art and imagining the finished
book on a shelf.

The backer is helping a creator prove that there is an audience. In return,
the backer usually gets the finished product, often at a campaign price, and
sometimes gets extras, early updates, digital files, or a say in how the
project develops. The creator gets money earlier than they would through
ordinary sales, which can pay for the things that make a game possible:
writing, editing, illustration, layout, printing, manufacturing, postage, and
the mysterious black art of getting several hundred boxes to the right houses.

That does not mean the creator has discovered a magic money tree. Kickstarter
fees, taxes, shipping, manufacturing costs, exchange rates, and the occasional
surprise hiding inside a spreadsheet all still exist. They may even become more
real after the campaign succeeds.

The campaign is therefore part pitch, part shop window, part development plan,
and part public promise. That is a lot to fit between a video, a reward table,
and a picture of a beautifully painted miniature.

## The TTRPG shelf is bigger because of this

The most obvious thing Kickstarter has brought to game development is a route
to making things that might otherwise be considered too specialised.

A traditional publisher has to make decisions about print runs, distribution,
cash flow, shelf space, and whether enough people will buy a product to justify
making it. That is perfectly understandable. Printers do not accept "but it is
really good" as a payment method.

Crowdfunding changes the first question. Instead of asking whether a publisher
can guess the market, a creator can ask the audience directly. That does not
remove the risk, but it can make a small audience useful. A project does not
need to be the next *Dungeons & Dragons* if it can find the people who want
that particular setting, rules system, adventure, magazine, set of miniatures,
or leather journal.

That is why the TTRPG category is more interesting than a list of products. It
shows the variety of things people are willing to support when the project is
explained clearly and the creator can reach the right audience.

It also means that "game development" is not just writing rules. It can include
building a physical space, making a useful accessory, commissioning a book of
art, producing a small magazine, or creating a supplement for a game that
already has a community around it.

## The campaigns I have backed

My own backing history is not a scientific sample. It is a record of what
caught my eye, what I thought I would use, and what I apparently decided I
needed while scrolling after tea.

I backed [Tiny Epic Dungeons Adventures](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/tinyepic/tiny-epic-dungeons-adventures),
which is a good example of an established game line using Kickstarter to
develop another substantial product for an audience that already knows the
name. There is a confidence loop there: the creator knows what sort of game it
is making, and the audience has some idea what sort of game it is backing.

I also backed [Arkand and the Book of Magic for the acclaimed Dragonbane
RPG](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/1192053011/arkand-and-the-book-of-magic-for-the-acclaimed-dragonbane-rpg).
This is the sort of project that benefits from an existing rules ecosystem.
The core game gives people a reason to care about a new book, while the new
book gives people another reason to keep playing the core game. I wrote about
why *Dragonbane* appealed to me in [my review of Dragonbane as a lighter
replacement for Dungeons & Dragons]({{ '/posts/2026/06/14/dragonbane-free-league-review.html' | relative_url }}),
and this is the more practical side of that same interest: what happens when
players want the game to grow?

[Dragonbane RPG Miniatures: Northern Beasts](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/titanforgeminis/dragonbane-rpg-miniatures-northern-beasts)
looks at the same wider ecosystem from another angle. Miniatures turn a set of
rules and creatures into physical objects. They are useful at the table, but
they are also designed things in their own right, with sculpting, moulds,
materials, painting, packing, and shipping waiting behind the attractive
photographs. A campaign can bring those two audiences together: people who
play the RPG and people who enjoy collecting the things that live around it.

[Shadowrun Anarchy 2.0](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/blackbook/shadowrun-anarchy-20)
is an example of a campaign arriving with a setting and history already behind
it. That can be an advantage because people understand the promise. It can
also create an enormous expectation that the new project will respect what
came before. This is where my older post on [game
licensing]({{ '/posts/2017/05/07/game-licensing.html' | relative_url }}) still
feels relevant. Working with an established property is not the same as
inventing a world in your shed and hoping somebody notices.

I backed [Secret Passages 3: An Old School RPG and Oldhammer
Magazine](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/jdhoare/secret-passages-3-an-old-school-rpg-and-oldhammer-magazine),
which represents the small-press end of the scale. A project does not have to
look like a giant boxed game to be worthwhile. A magazine, an adventure, or a
focused collection can be exactly the right size for a community. In some ways,
these are the projects that show crowdfunding at its most useful: enough
people want the thing, but perhaps not enough people are standing outside a
warehouse asking for it.

[Lunaria: Self-Standing Leather Journal for TTRPG and
Journaling](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/lunarchronicle/lunaria-self-standing-leather-journal-for-ttrpg-and-journaling)
is not a conventional RPG book at all. It is a physical object designed to sit
alongside play and writing. That matters because the TTRPG hobby is full of
things that support the game without being the game: notebooks, cards, maps,
tokens, dice, terrain, reference sheets, and the increasingly elaborate
apparatus required to remember what happened to the party last Tuesday.

[Grunks Tavern: Your Friendly Game and Coffee Experience
Shop](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/worldsataglance/grunks-tavern-your-friendly-game-and-coffee-experience-shop)
pushes the idea further still. The thing being funded is a place and an
experience, not merely a product that arrives in a cardboard box. A venue can
give people somewhere to play, meet, run events, and find the social side of
the hobby. That is a useful reminder that game development is also about
creating the conditions in which games happen.

I backed [A Show of Hands tabletop RPG](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/1769353621/a-show-of-hands-tabletop-rpg),
an independent RPG project, and [All for Anvil: An Adventure
Book](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/all-for-anvil/all-for-anvil-an-adventure-book).
These are good examples of how a campaign can give a specific idea a chance to
find its people. A new RPG or adventure book is competing for attention with
an awful lot of other things, but a well-made campaign can explain its tone,
show its work, and give potential players a reason to take a chance.

Finally, I backed [RPG Saints Sketchbook by Wayne
Reynolds](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/430117103/rpg-saints-sketchbook-by-wayne-reynolds).
That is a creator-led art book rather than a playable game, but it belongs in
the same conversation. RPG culture is supported by artists, illustrators,
cartographers, writers, editors, printers, and designers. Crowdfunding can let
an audience support the work of a particular creator directly, especially when
the value is in the person, the style, or the collection as much as in a set of
rules.

Taken together, these campaigns cover a surprisingly large part of the hobby.
There are games, supplements, miniatures, magazines, accessories, venues, and
art. Kickstarter has not simply made more rulebooks. It has made more ways to
participate in game development.

## Kickstarter as an MVP with better artwork

One of the more useful ideas from software and product development is the
Minimum Viable Product (MVP). The basic idea is to make the smallest useful
version of something, put it in front of people, and learn what happens before
spending years polishing the wrong thing.

Kickstarter is not automatically an MVP. Some campaigns launch with a very
large product and a very confident delivery plan. Still, the campaign can
work as a kind of public test. Do people understand the idea? Do they want it?
Which reward tiers make sense? Are people interested in a book, a box, a
digital version, or all three because apparently one format was not enough?

The danger is that the campaign can also encourage the opposite of an MVP.
Stretch goals multiply. Add-ons appear. A sensible book becomes a book, a
screen, a map pack, a miniature, a special edition, and a collector's box
that requires a forklift. The audience is excited, the funding total rises,
and the original production plan quietly leaves through the back door.

For creators, the discipline is in treating the campaign as a delivery
commitment rather than a popularity contest. More money is helpful. More
complexity is not always helpful. A successful campaign can still be a failed
development project if the creator has funded a product they cannot actually
make, pack, and send.

## The backer is part of the development story

Backing something early changes the relationship between creator and audience.
I am not just waiting for a finished game to appear on a shop shelf. I am
watching updates, looking at drafts, learning about delays, and occasionally
remembering that manufacturing is not a button marked "print".

That can be rewarding. It gives a sense of being near the work while it is
happening. It can also be uncomfortable. The creator has to communicate when
the plan changes, and the backer has to accept that a campaign is not the same
as buying a finished product from a shop.

That does not excuse poor communication or endless missed promises. It does
mean that the bargain needs to be understood. A pledge is a vote of confidence
with a product attached, not a guarantee that every photograph in the campaign
will arrive exactly as imagined on the original delivery month.

I have learned to look for evidence of a plan: clear production stages,
realistic shipping information, examples of finished work, and updates that
explain decisions rather than simply repeating that everything is exciting.
Excitement is good. A timetable is better.

## What it means for my own work

Kickstarter has made me think differently about publishing. I have written
about [digital RPG books and why Humble Bundle deserves your
attention]({{ '/posts/2026/05/05/digital-rpg-books-and-humble-bundle.html' | relative_url }})
and asked [whether I could put my own work on
DriveThruRPG]({{ '/posts/2026/11/02/getting-started-with-drivethrurpg.html' | relative_url }}).
Those routes have different pressures. A Kickstarter asks an audience to
commit before the product is finished. A storefront asks me to finish enough
of a product that somebody can buy it without needing to trust my production
schedule.

I am also reminded that making a game artefact is more than writing the words.
My [Homebrewery review]({{ '/posts/2026/10/25/homebrewery-dnd-artefact-review.html' | relative_url }})
was partly about the practical gap between having game material and having
something people can comfortably use. Kickstarter campaigns put that gap on
display. The pitch needs to explain the game, but it also needs to explain the
object, the process, and the route from idea to somebody's doormat.

That is probably why I find the TTRPG category so useful to browse. It is a
catalogue of finished ideas, certainly, but it is also a catalogue of
production decisions. What has somebody chosen to make? What have they left
out? What are they asking the audience to believe? How much of the campaign is
about the game, and how much is about making the creator's plan feel
trustworthy?

I am still not saying I will launch a Kickstarter tomorrow. I have seen enough
campaigns to know that "I have an idea" is not the same as "I have a fulfilment
plan". Also, my current desk is not rated for industrial quantities of boxes.

## So, has Kickstarter improved game development?

Yes, but not simply because it has created more games.

It has made it easier for some creators to find an audience before they have
access to traditional publishing money. It has allowed niche projects to be
measured against real interest rather than a publisher's best guess. It has
given artists, writers, designers, small publishers, and community builders a
way to explain a project directly to the people who might care about it.

It has also made the audience more aware of how games are made. We see the
costs, the production stages, the risks, and the decisions. Sometimes we see
too many updates about boxes. That is part of the education.

The model is not perfect. It rewards presentation, existing reach, and the
ability to keep attention for a few weeks. It can make backers carry more
risk than an ordinary customer would. It can encourage too much product, too
many rewards, and too much optimism. The best campaign pages in the world
cannot repeal the laws of manufacturing, postage, or arithmetic.

But for tabletop games, where communities are often passionate, specialised,
and willing to support a very particular thing, Kickstarter has clearly changed
what can be attempted. It has helped turn "there might be an audience for this"
into a question that can be asked directly.

That is a powerful change. It is also why I will probably continue browsing
the TTRPG category, carefully considering each campaign, and occasionally
pledging before breakfast.

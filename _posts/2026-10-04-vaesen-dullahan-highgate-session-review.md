I ran a short *Vaesen* mystery for Old Joe, Sidney, and Sabriel: three
investigators attached to the London house of the Society, near Highgate
Cemetery. It was dark, tense, and rather more straightforward to prepare than I
expected.

More usefully, it gave me a clear answer to one of the questions behind trying
*Vaesen*: can its folklore-investigation structure carry a session without a
large amount of plot written in advance?

For this session, I think the answer was yes.

## A hatbox for the Society

The mystery began with a delivery to the Society house. There was a plain
hatbox, a red ribbon, and a small card marked only with a black star.

One of the house staff opened the box. For her trouble, she was drenched in
blood. Inside was a skull, and nothing more.

I had based the moment on the Dullahan's folklore: someone who opens a door as
the headless rider passes can be drenched in blood. Moving that event into a
hatbox made the danger immediate and personal, while leaving the players with
questions rather than a fight.

The skull belonged to a Dullahan. In my version of the creature, the skull was
also its vulnerability: someone who seized it could command the Dullahan, and
destroying it would destroy the creature. The player characters did not know
this. They only knew that an unknown organisation, the Black Star, had sent a
dangerous object to the Society.

That distinction mattered. The Dullahan was not pursuing the investigators out
of personal malice. It wanted its head back. The Society, and therefore the
investigators, had become collateral damage in the Black Star's plan.

## Letting the clues create the plot

My preparation was deliberately small. I knew who had caused the problem, what
the creature wanted, what would happen if nothing interrupted it, and the
general atmosphere I wanted: dark, frightening, and increasingly urgent.

Everything else belonged to the table.

The players investigated through the strengths of their characters. They used
contacts, History, conversation, and the Society's resources to identify the
skull and learn enough of the Dullahan's nature to understand the danger. They
found traits and possible countermeasures, but did not discover the secret of
the skull before the creature arrived.

That did not feel like a failed investigation. It felt like the useful sort of
partial knowledge that gives a group something difficult to decide. They knew
enough to be afraid. They did not know enough to be comfortable.

This is where *Vaesen* worked well for me. A mystery does not need every clue
to point towards one prescribed scene. I could present the situation, answer
reasonable questions, and let the players make the connections. Their planning
became the story.

## A clock until sunset

I borrowed a [progress-clock idea from *Blades in the Dark*](https://bladesinthedark.com/progress-clocks)
to make the investigation's deadline visible. The skull arrived in
mid-afternoon, giving the players a safe period to research. Sunset was the
point at which the Dullahan could begin to close in.

The clock did two jobs. For me as GM, it was a simple measure of when it was
time to send the creature. For the players, it made the consequences of
successful and unsuccessful research physical and visible. Each result did not
only add information; it changed how much safety remained.

I liked that pressure because it did not tell the players what to do. It did
not say that they had to fight the Dullahan, hand over the skull, or solve the
Black Star's larger scheme immediately. It simply made delay expensive.

That is a very useful tool for this kind of horror. The threat was not an
abstract promise that something might happen later. It was coming after dark.

## A quieter confrontation

Once the group understood that the creature was on its way, they chose not to
meet it in the Society house. They lured it towards Highgate Cemetery, close
enough to be practical but safer than waiting in the building, left the skull
for it to reclaim, and observed rather than fought.

I liked that ending precisely because it was not a conventional final battle.
The players had assessed a frightening creature, admitted what they did not
know, and made a careful plan that protected the Society. The Dullahan got what
it wanted. The investigators survived to follow the Black Star's thread.

Old Joe, the former groundskeeper and gardener; Sidney, the street-urchin farm
boy; and Sabriel, the country-house maid and huntress mystic, all contributed
in ways that felt true to their characters. That is a good sign for a
one-shot: nobody needed to wait for the obvious monster hunter to solve the
case.

## Dullahan, not Sleepy Hollow

It is tempting to call any headless rider the Headless Horseman from *The
Legend of Sleepy Hollow*. The comparison is useful for atmosphere, but it is
not the same figure.

The Dullahan is an Irish folkloric death-omen: a headless rider or coachman
travelling at night, associated with the collecting of the doomed. Washington
Irving's Horseman is a specific literary ghost, a Hessian soldier said to have
lost his head during the American Revolutionary War. Both belong to the wider
family of headless-horseman stories, but I do not think it is accurate to say
that one is simply the other in a different costume.

For this game, the Dullahan's own folklore was more useful than the familiar
American ghost-story image. The blood, the missing head, and the sense of an
inevitable nocturnal visitor gave the mystery its tone before I needed to add
much else.

## What I learnt from the session

I would not treat one session as a complete review of *Vaesen*. We did not test
every part of the game, and the players did not need to resolve the Black Star
plot in a single evening.

But I do think it was a good demonstration of what the game is for. *Vaesen*
gave me a way to make folklore the practical engine of the scenario. The
investigators had to recognise a threat, ask the right questions, use their
contacts, and decide what mattered before the night ran out.

For a GM, that is an inviting proposition. I needed a charged opening
situation, a creature with clear needs, a small number of people behind the
problem, and a deadline. The players supplied the investigation, intrigue, and
strategy.

That is minimal preparation, but not empty preparation. It is enough material
to let the table discover the session for itself.

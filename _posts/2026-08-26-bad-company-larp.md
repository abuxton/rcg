I have had a "Bad Company LARP" file sat in my drafts since 2017. It contained
the title, some tags and absolutely no useful thoughts. That is not a design
document. It is, at best, evidence that I liked the idea.

I still do. [Bad Company](https://2000ad.com/characters/bad-company/) has the
sort of violent, strange military science-fiction atmosphere that could work
very well in a small Live Action Role Play (L(A)RP) event. I use L(A)RP as my
preferred term here; there is a little more on that in my
[glossary]({{ '/glossary/' | relative_url }}).

This would not be a game about asking players to portray the comic's established
characters. They belong to the comic, and trying to reproduce them exactly
would make the event narrower rather than richer. I would much rather play a
different intake of recruits: people who have heard the stories, perhaps who
served alongside somebody like Mad Tommy, but who have their own very short
chance to become legends.

## The references are a mood board, not a plot

The comic is the obvious starting point, but I would also look to
[*Aliens*](https://www.20thcenturystudios.com/movies/aliens) and
[*Outpost*](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Film/Outpost) for the
feeling of a small unit being sent somewhere it really ought not to be. The
former gives me exhausted soldiers, industrial spaces and a team that has to
work together. The latter is useful for its grim, enclosed pressure.

For the staging, I would borrow from [neon noir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neon_noir):
hard pools of coloured light, deep shadows, wet-looking surfaces and
silhouettes. That does not mean every field needs to become a cyberpunk film
set. A few practical lamps, coloured gels, a dark corridor and careful control
of what is visible can do far more than trying to light an entire site.

The point is not to reenact a particular strip. It is to make the players feel
like a group of recruits who have been dropped into the same sort of brutal
universe: under-equipped, uncertain what their commanders know, and very aware
that the briefing may have left out the important bit.

## Small field, small player group

I think this concept becomes less interesting as it gets bigger. A small group
of perhaps eight to sixteen players, plus crew, means each absence is noticed.
If one person is separated from the squad, everyone feels the missing body.
If the medic is hit, it becomes a problem rather than an announcement over a
radio.

The field of play should be equally contained. A woodland boundary, a small
industrial-style airsoft site, or a handful of connected rooms gives the crew
control over distance and information. It also prevents the game becoming a
long walk between occasional firefights. The squad needs to believe it can see
the way out, while being unable to reach it safely.

That scale also makes a first run realistic. I would rather make one defended
compound, one dangerous route and one objective work properly than promise a
planetary war and deliver a few disconnected skirmishes.

## Airsoft or laser tag, with atmosphere doing the heavy lifting

The science-fiction setting invites either airsoft or laser-tag combat. Which
one I chose would depend on the venue, the people involved and the safety
framework, rather than on a claim that one is more authentic. Airsoft has the
useful physicality of cover, magazines and uncertainty. Laser tag can make
night play and obvious futuristic equipment much easier to run.

Neither is the game on its own. Clear briefings, marshals, safe prop standards
and a written combat policy would matter more than the choice of replica. I
would need to decide how hits work before writing the plot: immediate return to
base, a limited wound state, or a short chance for a squadmate to recover
someone. The answer changes the whole pace of play.

Weather could be an ally, within sensible safety limits. Rain on a corrugated
roof, mud under boots and a cold evening all help make a tiny site feel
hostile. I would not rely on weather, or deliberately run people into unsafe
conditions, but I would design the event so that an overcast day adds texture
rather than ruining the plan.

Similarly, smoke and lighting need restraint. A small amount of venue-approved
atmospheric smoke, used where it cannot hide trip hazards or obscure exits,
could make a short approach genuinely tense. Light should direct attention,
not make players unable to see their feet. These are tools for staging scenes,
not excuses to make an unsafe field.

## A game where death is expected

The most interesting design problem is that Bad Company feels like a setting
where people die. That does not mean a L(A)RP should casually remove players
from their only activity. It means being honest, before booking, that
casualties are part of the premise and making a replacement or post-death role
interesting.

For a one-day game, I would set expectations in the booking information and
the opening briefing: a character might not make it to the final objective.
When that happens, the player should have somewhere to go. They might return
as a wounded recruit with limited information, join a small opposing force, or
play a newly arrived replacement who has missed the camaraderie that made the
first squad work.

A twelve-hour event could make this rhythm more deliberate. The early phase
builds the unit through routine patrols and poor jokes; the middle removes
certainty; the final few hours ask whoever is left to decide what the mission
was worth. A high fatality expectation is only dramatic if the players have
time to care about the people being lost.

I would make the end condition survival-adjacent rather than a simple body
count. Extracting intelligence, getting one person through, or deciding not to
follow an impossible order can all be meaningful outcomes. The recruits may
not win, but their choices should still matter.

## The first prototype

The sensible first version is not a campaign. It is a short test: a single
squad sent to investigate a silent relay station, with one route in, one
unreliable contact and a clear extraction point. The crew could test the
combat rules, the lighting and the replacement-character process without
needing an entire fictional war behind them.

If that works, there is space for a series of linked recruit groups: an earlier
intake, the survivors of a failed mission, or people who once served with Mad
Tommy and now have a story they are not sure they believe. That gives the
source material room to cast a shadow without requiring anyone to play the
comic's protagonists.

This is still only a design idea, and any public use of the setting would need
the appropriate permission from its rights holders. But as a small, intense
fan-event concept, I think it has more life in it than that empty 2017 draft
suggested.

I am writing this in July 2026, which feels like an appropriate moment. New *Ravenloft*
material has arrived, and it gives the series a present-tense anchor. But before I get to
that, I want to think about what happened when the setting came back under 5th edition.

## Curse of Strahd: the return to Barovia

When Wizards of the Coast announced *Curse of Strahd* in 2016, it was positioned as a return
to the original adventure rather than a full setting revival. The decision made sense. Rather
than attempting to revive the entire Demiplane of Dread immediately, they went back to the
source: Barovia, Strahd, Castle Ravenloft, and the Gothic horror premise that made the
original *I6* module distinctive in the first place.

*Curse of Strahd* is, in many ways, an expansion and refinement of the original module's
design. The card-draw randomisation returns — the Tarokka deck reading that determines where
key items are hidden, what Strahd's current fixation is, and which ally will aid the party.
That mechanic gives the campaign genuine replayability and was, I think, a deliberate act of
homage to the Hickmans' original design. The adventure does not pretend the 1983 module did
not exist; it treats it as the foundation and builds on it.

The execution was widely well-received, and I think it earned that reception. The adventure is
not a conservative exercise. It expanded Barovia into a full-scale campaign region, developed
Strahd into a genuinely complex antagonist rather than simply a powerful monster, and
incorporated a level of emotional and narrative texture that distinguished it from the
typical dungeon-crawl adventure. The setting materials for the village of Vallaki, the
Vistani, and the various allies and complications give a GM real material to work with.

Whether it captured the original Gothic horror atmosphere of the second edition campaign
setting is a different question. *Curse of Strahd* is excellent 5th edition *Dungeons &
Dragons*. It is atmospheric and well-designed. Whether it is genuinely Gothic in the way the
Hickmans intended is, I think, something each table will answer differently depending on how
they run it.

## Curse of Strahd Revamped

*Curse of Strahd: Revamped* appeared in 2020 as a premium edition of the same adventure
with revised content, updated maps, and additional material including a *Tarokka* deck as a
physical inclusion. It was not a fundamental reimagining — the core adventure remained the same
— but it addressed some of the original release's shortcomings around the Vistani
representation, which had attracted legitimate criticism. The revisions were made thoughtfully,
in my view, and improved the product.

I do think the premium presentation matters for a setting like *Ravenloft*. The visual and
tactile elements of play — the card deck, the handouts, the atmospheric staging — are part of
how Gothic horror operates at the table. Gothic fiction is about sensation and atmosphere as
much as plot, and a well-produced physical product supports that.

## Van Richten's Guide to Ravenloft: the wider Demiplane returns

The more significant development came in 2021 with *Van Richten's Guide to Ravenloft*. Where
*Curse of Strahd* had focused on Barovia, *Van Richten's Guide* opened up the full concept of
the Demiplane of Dread: multiple domains, multiple Darklords, a wider toolkit for horror
campaigns across very different registers.

This was the book that told me Wizards of the Coast were taking the setting seriously rather
than treating it as a one-off nostalgia product. The decision to expand beyond Barovia — to
revive the idea that the Mists could contain Gothic horror, cosmic horror, body horror,
psychological horror, and folk horror all within the same framework — was the move that the
second edition setting had been founded on. The return to that structural openness matters.

The book also updated the moral architecture. The Darklords of *Van Richten's Guide* are
defined by contemporary sensibilities around trauma and harm as well as the classical Gothic
model of damnation and obsession. Whether that update strengthens or dilutes the original
premise is, in my honest opinion, a question that depends heavily on the specific domain in
question. Some of the new domains feel genuinely fresh; others feel like horror-genre exercises
with a thin connection to the Demiplane's deeper themes. That is, I suppose, an inevitable
consequence of breadth.

What I will say without reservation is that *Van Richten's Guide* is an excellent book for GMs
who want to run horror campaigns within *Dungeons & Dragons*. The toolkit it provides —
the horror genre guidance, the domain structure, the advice on safety and consent at the table
in horror-adjacent play — is genuinely useful independent of how attached you are to the
*Ravenloft* brand.

## June 2026: Dungeons & Dragons Ravenloft: The Horrors Within

And now June 2026 has brought new material: *Dungeons & Dragons Ravenloft: The Horrors Within*.

I am not going to pretend I have read it cover to cover at the time of writing — it is newly
released and my copy is recent. What I can say is what the release signals.

The fact that Wizards of the Coast have committed to a new *Ravenloft* title less than five
years after *Van Richten's Guide* suggests confidence in the setting's commercial and creative
viability within the current edition. It also suggests they believe there is more to say within
the Demiplane of Dread than the previous releases covered.

From the available information, *The Horrors Within* continues the work *Van Richten's Guide*
began: expanding the Demiplane, developing domains and Darklords further, and providing
material for GMs who want to run sustained Gothic horror campaigns rather than one-off
encounters with vampires and fog. That trajectory is, in my view, the right one.

The original *Ravenloft* was a setting about the nature of damnation — about what it costs to
want the wrong thing, to fail at the crucial moment, or to become something monstrous in the
pursuit of something worthwhile. The Darklords were not simply powerful antagonists; they were
cautionary figures with genuine interiority. If *The Horrors Within* develops that tradition,
it will be doing what *Ravenloft* does best.

I will write a fuller response once I have spent more time with the material. For now, the
series closes here: a setting that started as a single module in 1983, became a full campaign
world, survived the d20 transition through an unconventional collaboration, weathered a
fourth-edition silence, came back to life under 5th edition, and has now produced new material
four decades after a Welsh couple wrote a vampire into a castle in Barovia and changed what
horror could mean in a tabletop game.

That is a long shadow to cast from a cardboard module.

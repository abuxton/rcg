The third version of the auction removes the party from both the guard rota and
the burglary plan. Each player arrives as a representative: perhaps a trusted
agent, perhaps a hired sword with a letter of credit, perhaps the only person
their absent patron trusts not to embarrass them in public.

I call this version *Dialling It In*. The name is deliberately a little silly,
but the idea behind it is serious. A proxy has two loyalties: the people in
the room and the person who sent them there. That tension is enough to make
the auction itself an adventure rather than a scene between adventures.

## Money is only one kind of leverage

Every representative needs a private brief. One patron wants an item at any
cost; another wants to stop a rival buying it; a third is there to learn who
is funding the sale. A player can be given a budget, a reputation to protect
and one instruction that becomes awkward once they meet the other guests.

I would avoid making an auction a long spreadsheet of rising numbers. A bid
can instead be a moment of social pressure. Who looks confident? Who is
bluffing? Who can call in a favour, expose a weakness or persuade the room
that an apparently desirable lot is trouble? A light social mechanic can help
when the table wants it, but roleplay should always be able to carry the
scene.

The best outcome is not necessarily taking the prize home. A representative
who loses an object but wins an ally, uncovers a patron's identity or prevents
the wrong buyer from succeeding has still played a good game.

## Why it suits a small parlour L(A)RP

This is also the version that most naturally becomes a parlour Live Action
Role Play (L(A)RP). One dressed room, a few clearly labelled props, invitations
and short private briefs can create a great deal of play. The public auction
provides a rhythm; the conversations in the corners provide the plot.

I would keep a first event small and make the boundaries explicit. Guests need
to know whether the game permits physical contact, theft of props, private
conversation, alcohol and any form of conflict. My default would be no physical
combat, no real valuables in play and a simple way to pause or step out of a
scene. A [Session Zero]({{ '/2023/12/03/session-zero/' | relative_url }})-style
briefing is just as useful for a one-evening L(A)RP as it is for a campaign.

Costume and food are atmosphere, not entry requirements. A table of mock lots,
name cards and a confident auctioneer will do more than asking people to spend
money on an outfit. The important prop is the private instruction that makes
two friendly people suddenly need incompatible things.

## The development lesson

An auction gives a GM or organiser a useful public structure. Everybody knows
when the next lot will be called. Everybody is free between lots to scheme,
trade information and make promises they may not keep. It is an excellent
frame for players who enjoy character interaction but still want the evening
to move somewhere.

patron's credit note: powerful adventurers invited to the sale in their own
right.

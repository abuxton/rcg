There is a particular kind of excitement in pressing the button that makes a
thing public. The player primer for *The Auction* is close enough to a
publication that I have started thinking about its product page, its cover and
how it might look on DriveThruRPG.

I am not pressing that button yet.

This is the next step in the small publishing experiment I have been writing
about. [The Homebrewery review]({{ '/posts/2026/10/25/homebrewery-dnd-artefact-review.html' | relative_url }})
looked at turning a draft into something that resembles a finished game
handout. [Getting started with DriveThruRPG]({{ '/posts/2026/11/02/getting-started-with-drivethrurpg.html' | relative_url }})
looked at the practical requirements and the difference between having a
publisher account and having permission to use somebody else's game world.
Then [the player primer post]({{ '/posts/2026/11/15/the-auction-player-primer.html' | relative_url }})
shared the current Homebrewery draft.

Those posts were not a disguised launch campaign. They were a trail of
questions. The answer I have reached, for now, is that the primer is
interesting, useful and incomplete.

## A draft can look more finished than it is

The current primer has a shape. It gives players a reason to be in the room,
some possible roles and relationships, a little etiquette and a few prompts
for starting conversations. It tries to work for a tabletop Role Playing Game
(RPG) and for a smaller parlour Live Action Role Play (L(A)RP) game without
pretending that those are identical experiences.

Homebrewery makes that shape visible. Headings become pages, columns create
the rhythm of a booklet and a contents page makes the material feel like a
publication. That is valuable work. It also makes the gaps easier to see.

A good-looking page is not the same thing as a good player resource. The
primer needs more depth before I can ask a stranger to pay for it, download it
and bring it to a table. It needs clearer examples of what players can do with
the prompts, more ways to adapt the material to different groups and enough
practical detail that the document remains useful after the first read.

It also needs another pass for the less visible parts of publication:
consistent terminology, readable page breaks, accessible typography, links
that work in the exported PDF, and a careful check that player information
does not accidentally become Game Master information. A preview is evidence
that the layout is possible. It is not evidence that the book is ready.

## The work is in the conversations

The missing material is not just a list of paragraphs I can write in an empty
room. It needs interaction.

I want to put the primer in front of people who would actually play the
auction. Which prompts make them want to talk? Which ones are too vague? Does
the distinction between a character, a proxy and a patron make sense without
the rest of the series beside it? Does the safety guidance help a group make
decisions, or does it disappear into the page?

Those are questions for human readers and players. A playtest can show that a
section is technically clear but socially awkward. A conversation can reveal
that a phrase assumes more genre knowledge than I intended. An editor can spot
that three different ideas are using the same word. None of those discoveries
comes from making the text longer. They come from seeing how other people
understand it.

The next version therefore needs to grow through review, not simply through
generation. I need to write more of the material myself, ask other people to
challenge it, revise it and then read it again as somebody arriving at the
auction for the first time.

## Artwork is part of the decision

The draft has no illustrations. That is obvious as soon as the cover
placeholders appear, but leaving a page empty is preferable to filling it
with artwork that does not belong there.

I am not going to use AI-generated artwork in the primer. That is not a
temporary omission while I find a better prompt. If the finished publication
has artwork, I want it to come from a human artist or from a source whose
licence clearly permits this use. The artist should be credited, the licence
should be recorded and the images should contribute something to the setting
rather than merely decorate a spare corner.

That means the art is another part of the work still to do. I need to decide
what the primer actually needs, find suitable work, check the permissions and
make the layout fit the images rather than treating them as interchangeable
texture. It may turn out that a restrained, mostly typographic booklet is
better than a rushed illustrated one. That is a creative decision, not a
failure to complete the asset list.

## What AI is useful for here

I am happy to use artificial intelligence (AI) tools where they help with the
mechanical parts of the process. They can help explore layout options, check
typography, find inconsistent punctuation, identify broken references and
suggest places where a paragraph needs another edit. They can be useful during
development and review, provided I remain responsible for deciding what
belongs in the document.

That is different from handing over the creative centre of the primer. The
majority of the writing needs human interaction: deciding what players should
know, choosing the tone, inventing the invitations and boundaries, listening
to feedback and making the final call. The artwork should be sourced, credited
and licensed, not generated.

For me, this is a useful boundary. Tools can reduce friction around production
without becoming the author of the experience. They can help me see a draft
more clearly, but they cannot replace the people who will play it, question it
or make it meaningful.

## So, no DriveThruRPG listing yet

The primer is not being published on DriveThruRPG this Sunday. It is not ready,
and describing it as ready would turn a useful experiment into a misleading
product promise.

The research in [issue #79](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/79) made the
other part of the decision clearer too. A marketplace account is not a
shortcut around intellectual-property permissions, content requirements or
production quality. If this eventually becomes a listing, I will need to
recheck the current platform guidance, confirm the route and rights that apply,
and make sure the product is genuinely complete.

For now, the Homebrewery draft remains a draft. The next milestone is not
uploading it. It is expanding the material, testing it with people, improving
the writing, sourcing appropriate artwork and reviewing the whole thing as a
publication rather than a promising set of pages.

That feels like a slower ending to this series. It is probably a better one.

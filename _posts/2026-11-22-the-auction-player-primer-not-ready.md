There is a dangerous moment in any small publishing project. It is the point
where the draft has a cover page, a contents page and enough tidy headings to
start looking at you as if it expects a product description.

The player primer for *The Auction* has reached that point. It is close enough
to a publication that I have started thinking about its cover, its product
page and how it might look on DriveThruRPG.

It is not ready. I am not pressing the button.

That is not a dramatic refusal to publish. It is the much less glamorous
decision to admit that a document can look like a book while still having the
editorial structural integrity of a stack of takeaway menus.

This is the next step in the small publishing experiment I have been writing
about. [The Homebrewery review]({{ '/posts/2026/10/25/homebrewery-dnd-artefact-review.html' | relative_url }})
looked at turning a draft into something that resembles a finished game
handout. [Getting started with DriveThruRPG]({{ '/posts/2026/11/02/getting-started-with-drivethrurpg.html' | relative_url }})
looked at the practical requirements, and at the important difference between
having a publisher account and having permission to use somebody else's game
world. Then [the player primer post]({{ '/posts/2026/11/15/the-auction-player-primer.html' | relative_url }})
shared the current Homebrewery draft.

Those posts were not a disguised launch campaign. They were a trail of
questions, with a few attractive page breaks along the way. The answer I have
reached, for now, is that the primer is interesting, useful and incomplete.

## A draft can look more finished than it is

The current primer has a shape. It gives players a reason to be in the room,
some possible roles and relationships, a little etiquette and a few prompts
for starting conversations. It tries to work for a tabletop Role Playing Game
(RPG) and for a smaller parlour Live Action Role Play (L(A)RP) game without
pretending that those are identical experiences.

Homebrewery makes that shape very easy to see. Headings become pages, columns
give the booklet a rhythm and a contents page arrives wearing the confident
expression of a document that has definitely been proofread. This is useful
work. It also shines a small, unforgiving lamp into the gaps.

A good-looking page is not the same thing as a good player resource. The
primer needs more depth before I can ask somebody I have never met to pay for
it, download it and bring it to a table. It needs clearer examples of what
players can do with the prompts, more ways to adapt the material to different
groups and enough practical detail that the document remains useful after the
first read.

At the moment, some of the material is still closer to an invitation than a
proper set of directions. That can be a strength at the table, but it can
also leave a player wondering whether they have been given a hook, a
suggestion or an unfinished sentence wearing a nice hat.

There is the less visible work as well: consistent terminology, readable page
breaks, accessible typography, links that work in the exported PDF, and a
careful check that player information does not quietly turn into Game Master
information. A preview proves that the layout is possible. It does not prove
that the publication is ready to be let out without a lead.

## The work is in the conversations

The missing material is not just a list of paragraphs I can write in an empty
room. It needs interaction. Preferably the useful kind, rather than a
conversation with myself in which every draft is declared brilliant.

I want to put the primer in front of people who would actually play the
auction. Which prompts make them want to talk? Which ones are too vague? Does
the distinction between a character, a proxy and a patron make sense without
the rest of the series beside it? Does the safety guidance help a group make
decisions, or does it vanish into the page like a badly labelled prop?

Those are questions for human readers and players. A playtest can show that a
section is technically clear but socially awkward. A conversation can reveal
that a phrase assumes more genre knowledge than I intended. An editor can
spot that three different ideas are using the same word, which is the sort of
thing that seems harmless until six players are all confidently doing
different things.

None of those discoveries comes from making the text longer. They come from
seeing how other people understand it. The next version needs to grow through
review, not simply through generation. I need to write more of the material
myself, ask other people to challenge it, revise it and then read it again as
somebody arriving at the auction for the first time.

That is also where the human creativity sits for me. The primer is not just a
container for information about an evening. It is an invitation to imagine a
role, make a connection and decide what matters when the bidding starts to
go sideways. Those choices need people in the room, not just a fluent
paragraph generator.

## Artwork is part of the decision

The draft has no illustrations. That becomes obvious as soon as the cover
placeholders appear, although the placeholders are at least honest. They do
not pretend that a blank page is a mysterious relic from the auction
catalogue.

I am not going to use AI-generated artwork in the primer. That is not a
temporary omission while I find a better prompt. If the finished publication
has artwork, I want it to come from a human artist or from a source whose
licence clearly permits this use. The artist should be credited, the licence
should be recorded and the images should contribute something to the setting
rather than merely decorate a spare corner.

That means the art is another part of the work still to do. I need to decide
what the primer actually needs, find suitable work, check the permissions and
make the layout fit the images rather than treating them as interchangeable
texture. It may turn out that a restrained, mostly typographic booklet is
better than a rushed illustrated one. That is a creative decision, not a
failure to complete the asset list.

I have opened a follow-up research task, [Research artwork sources and artist
recruitment](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/94), to investigate open
and permissively licensed artwork, ways to commission human artists and the
press or creator resources offered by game developers. The aim is to record
permissions and provenance before any image becomes part of the publication.

## What AI is useful for here

I am happy to use artificial intelligence (AI) tools where they help with the
mechanical parts of the process. They can help explore layout options, check
typography, find inconsistent punctuation, identify broken references and
point at places where a paragraph needs another edit. This is useful work.
It is also the sort of work that does not require me to pretend a machine has
joined the writing group and brought snacks.

The important distinction is between reducing production friction and handing
over the creative centre of the primer. The majority of the writing needs
human interaction: deciding what players should know, choosing the tone,
inventing the invitations and boundaries, listening to feedback and making
the final call. The artwork should be sourced, credited and licensed, not
generated.

For me, that is a useful boundary. Tools can help me see a draft more clearly
without becoming the author of the experience. They can check the commas
while people decide why the auction matters, what the players are allowed to
feel and how much trouble fits comfortably in one evening.

## So, no DriveThruRPG listing yet

The primer is not being published on DriveThruRPG this Sunday. It is not
ready, and describing it as ready would turn a useful experiment into a
misleading product promise. Nobody needs another PDF that confidently
announces itself as finished and then falls apart when somebody tries to use
it.

The research in [issue #79](https://github.com/abuxton/rcg/issues/79) made the
other part of the decision clearer too. A marketplace account is not a
shortcut around intellectual-property permissions, content requirements or
production quality. If this eventually becomes a listing, I will need to
recheck the current platform guidance, confirm the route and rights that
apply, and make sure the product is genuinely complete.

For now, the Homebrewery draft remains a draft. The next milestone is not
uploading it. It is expanding the material, testing it with people, improving
the writing, sourcing appropriate artwork and reviewing the whole thing as a
publication rather than a promising set of pages.

That feels like a slower ending to this series. It is probably a better one.
The auction can wait. It has already spent most of its life making people
wait in a queue.

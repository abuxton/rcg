I began with a one-page adventure about an auction house under pressure. I end
with the same building seen from four different positions: the people keeping
order, the people breaking in, the people bidding for absent masters, and the
people wealthy enough to be invited themselves.

That is my *layer cake* of the auction. It is not an attempt to replace or
expand the original into a definitive module. It is a reminder to myself that
a compact scenario can be a strong foundation precisely because it has not
answered every question.

## The four layers

1. [**The original review**]({{ '/posts/2026/09/02/the-dwarven-auction-one-page-review.html' | relative_url }})
   looks at the security-job premise: a one-hour event, a small plan and
   several motives arriving in sequence.
2. [**The heist**]({{ '/posts/2026/09/09/the-auction-as-a-heist.html' | relative_url }})
   uses *Keys from the Golden Vault* as a high-level touchstone for planning,
   preparation, non-violent options and consequences.
3. [**The proxy and parlour L(A)RP**]({{ '/posts/2026/09/16/the-auction-proxies-and-parlour-larp.html' | relative_url }})
   makes social leverage the engine, using the contrast between a formal sale
   and private agendas as its inspiration for bids that mean more than price.
4. [**The patrons**]({{ '/posts/2026/09/23/the-auction-for-established-adventurers.html' | relative_url }})
   asks what changes when the player characters have status, resources and
   enough power that social consequences matter more than a lock.

Each layer needs a different briefing, but the core remains recognisable:
valuable things, constrained space, a public timetable and people who want
incompatible outcomes. That is an admirably efficient adventure machine.

## What I would keep

The first lesson is to preserve the original's clarity. I would not add a
sub-basement, three rival guilds and a catalogue of twenty magic items merely
because I can. Every addition should answer a play question: does it give the
heist crew another approach, give a proxy a hard choice, or give a powerful
party a consequence they cannot simply overpower?

The second is to decide where the pressure comes from. Guards need incidents
and a clock. Thieves need incomplete information and escalation. Proxies need
secrets and social friction. Patrons need histories that make the lots matter.
Trying to run all four kinds of pressure at once is a fine way to make a small
scenario feel unmanageably busy.

Finally, an L(A)RP version needs care rather than extra rules. Brief people
well, set safety and prop boundaries, keep physical conflict out unless the
venue and organisers are equipped for it, and make sure every player has
something they can actively pursue. A parlour game works when its guests have
reasons to talk to one another, not when the room has enough fake treasure.

## Design principles across the series

This *Auction* series demonstrates how a single compact premise can support
diverse play styles without losing coherence. Each post builds on the insights
of previous posts, exploring the same venue and situation from different angles:
security, theft, proxy politics, high-level play, and finally an organized
L(A)RP event. The design lessons apply whether you're running an RPG session,
organizing a heist game, or hosting a parlour event.

they do not reproduce its text, artwork or map.

I also want to credit the **[One Page Dungeon
Contest](https://www.dungeoncontest.com/)**, whose celebration of concise,
playable adventures is a useful corrective whenever my own notes start
sprawling. One page can be enough to start an evening. With care, it can also
be enough to start a campaign, a heist or a very good night of L(A)RP.

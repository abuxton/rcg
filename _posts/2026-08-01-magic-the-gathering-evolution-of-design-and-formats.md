I have been circling this post for a while. *Magic: The Gathering* isn't a
tabletop Role Playing Game (RPG) or a Live (Action) Role Play (L(A)RP), but it
sits so close to the game design side of this blog that I couldn't leave it
out. It is also, quietly, one of the most instructive case studies in
long-term game design and product management I know of. Wizards of the Coast
have been iterating on the same core game for over thirty years, in public,
with a paper trail. That is a rare thing to be able to study.

This post is a summary and review of that evolution, from Richard Garfield's
original kitchen-table design through to the dense, format-heavy competitive
ecosystem that exists today. It is also, honestly, a bit of a lament. I miss
the simple days.

## Summary: how the design actually changed

Wizards' own "Making Magic" column (written by former head designer Mark
Rosewater) is the best primary source for this, and his ["30 years, part
1"](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/30-years-part-1) and
["Stages of
Design"](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/stages-of-design)
articles lay out the throughline clearly.

*Magic* didn't start as a competitive game. Garfield designed *Alpha* the way
you'd design any $20–$30 game bought at a local store: a self-contained
experience for a small playgroup, expected to be supplemented occasionally
with a booster pack. The rules were loose by design, because Garfield expected
arguments about how cards interacted to be resolved at the table, the same way
his inspiration *Cosmic Encounter* generated arguments. Ante — literally
wagering cards — was baked into the original rules. This was a game designed
for a kitchen table, not a tournament hall.

Rosewater identifies several distinct "stages" of design since then, each tied
to a different head designer and a different design priority:

- **Stage one (Alpha–Alliances):** individual cards had to be exciting enough
  to make people want to buy, collect, and trade them. The card *was* the
  product.
- **Later stages:** design shifted toward sets built for Limited play
  (*Mirage* onward), then blocks designed as connected multi-set stories,
  cross-set callbacks (the "Atog" cycle Rosewater describes is a lovely
  example of designers talking to their own future selves), and eventually
  "enters the battlefield" effects to solve card-file space problems.

What stands out reading this history is how much of it is standard product
iteration: identify a constraint, design around it, ship, observe, repeat.
Blocks were introduced, then later abandoned because they'd stopped serving
the game. That is healthy design maturity, not a failure. It is also exactly
the kind of "MVP, iterate, version" thinking that turns up elsewhere in
software and game development, and it's satisfying to see it named so
explicitly by the people doing it.

## Review: from casual to constructed, and the format explosion

The flip side of that design maturity is the [formats
page](https://magic.wizards.com/en/formats) Wizards now maintains, and the
sprawling breakdown sites like Game Kastle have to write just to explain it
all — see their ["All Magic: The Gathering Formats
Explained"](https://gamekastle.com/blogs/game-kastle-news/all-magic-the-gathering-formats-explained).
Standard, Pioneer, Modern, Legacy, Vintage, Pauper, Commander, Draft, Sealed,
Historic, Alchemy, Brawl, and a rotating cast of casual variants documented as
far back as Wizards' own [2008 casual formats
feature](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/casual-formats-2008-08-11)
— Mini-Master, Solomon Draft, Cube, and endless kitchen-table house rules.

Structurally, this is impressive. Wizards have built an entire taxonomy of
ways to play the same 20,000+ cards, each format designed to manage power
creep, card legality, and deck-building constraints for a different audience.
Commander in particular deserves credit for turning "too many old cards
lying around" into a thriving, social, singleton format that keeps decades-old
cards relevant.

But taken as a whole, this is also a formats *industry*. Legality is now a
constant background calculation. Is this card legal in Standard? Was it
rotated out last year? Is it banned in Modern? Do I need the Commander-legal
version? Every format has its own banned list, its own power ceiling, its own
economy of "staples" that shift in secondary-market price depending on
tournament results. Casual play increasingly means picking a format from a
menu rather than just... playing.

## Discussion: what I actually miss

This is where I step out of reviewer mode and into my own opinion, because
this is a blog about how I feel about game design, not a neutral encyclopaedia
entry.

I miss [Revised Edition](https://mtg.fandom.com/wiki/Revised_Edition).

Not because it was well made — by most accounts the print quality was rough,
the card pool was a mess of power outliers, and the rules text was
inconsistent by modern standards. I miss what it represented as a *play
experience*. You picked up a clean Revised starter box, grabbed a booster or
two, and you went and played with friends on a Friday night. That was it. No
format to choose. No banned list to check. No secondary market to consult
before you were "allowed" to bring a card to the table. No sense that the deck
in your hand was a competitive statement about your willingness to spend
money.

There was no pay-to-win ladder, because there was barely a ladder at all.
There was no constraint on discovery in your packs, because discovery *was*
the game — cracking a booster and figuring out, with your friends, whether
Shivan Dragon was actually good, was the entire appeal. There was no draft
build, no Commander deck, no sideboard plan against the field. There was just
whatever was in your box, whatever you'd traded for, and an evening with
people you liked.

I recognise the argument against this position. Loose rules and ante-based
play didn't scale. A game with a genuine professional circuit, a healthy
secondary market, and Commander tables at every game store needed the format
structure it built. Rosewater's own account makes clear that most of these
changes were necessary responses to real problems — Limited needed sets
designed for it, competitive play needed rotation and banned lists, casual
players needed something (Commander) that let old cards matter again. None of
this happened by accident or out of greed alone. It happened because the
original kitchen-table design genuinely could not support the game *Magic*
became.

But I'm allowed to miss it anyway. Good design maturity for a company running
a thirty-year-old product line is not always the same thing as good design
nostalgia for a player who just wants to sit down and play something. Somewhere
between Alpha's chaotic ante rules and today's format matrix, there was a
window — Revised, Fourth Edition, the early expansions — where you could hold
the entire legal card pool for casual play in a shoebox, and nobody needed to
check anything before you sat down. I think about that window a lot when I'm
designing tabletop and L(A)RP material of my own: the temptation to add
"just one more system" to solve "just one more problem" is exactly how you end
up thirty years later explaining formats to newcomers with a diagram.

## Where that leaves me

I don't think *Magic* made the wrong choices. I think it made the choices a
game of its scale and longevity had to make, and Rosewater's design writing is
some of the most transparent, thoughtful product-design commentary I've read
from inside any long-running franchise. But my own preference, for my own
Friday nights, remains the shoebox and the booster pack. Pure play, no
constraint on discovery, no ladder to climb. Just cards, friends, and
whatever you happened to crack open.
